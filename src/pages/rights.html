Who this works for

Workler is paid for by the person using it. There is no employer, recruiter or advertiser revenue, so no part of the product is optimised for anyone but the job seeker. Workler never submits an application, never sends a message, and never invents a line for your CV. Whether EU AI Act rules classify candidate-side tools remains unsettled.

One customer

In this category the job seeker is usually not the customer. Boards are paid by employers. Agencies are paid by employers. Free tools are paid by whoever is buying the data.

Workler is paid by you, and by nobody else.

That is not a promise about our intentions. It is a structural fact with a consequence you can check: there are no employer-side features, because there is no employer-side customer to build them for. The repository is open, and an employer dashboard is not a thing that can be hidden in it.

Three things it will not do

It will not send anything. Workler drafts your CV, your covering letter, your form answers and your follow-ups. You send them. There is a test in the repository that fails if this ever stops being true. Receipt → the submit-guard test

It will not invent anything. It reorders, reframes and emphasises what is already in your CV. It does not add an achievement you did not describe or a tool you did not use. Receipt → the instruction file

It will not flatter you. Below 4.0 out of 5 it recommends you don’t apply. A tool that never returns a bad score is not measuring anything. Receipt → the evaluation mode

What we don’t know yet

The EU AI Act treats employment-related AI as high-risk. The rules were written with employers and recruiters in view — the systems that filter candidates — and whether a tool used by the candidate falls inside them is not settled.

We are not going to put a compliance badge on that. When our lawyers answer it, the answer will be on this page with a date on it, and if the answer is that Workler is in scope, we will say so and say what changed.
